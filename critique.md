# Constructive review of the ICA–SAE reproduction

This project identifies an important issue in the original Figure 2 evaluation: ICA components are identifiable only up to sign, while the released interpretation procedure ranks fragments by their largest positive activation. The reported ICA score can therefore depend on an arbitrary sign chosen by the fitting algorithm. We appreciate that the project isolates this issue and shows that orienting ICA components can substantially narrow the measured interpretability gap.

Many aspects of the experiment—including evaluating the first 150 features, using an overcomplete SAE with \(R=2\), top-and-random scoring, and reporting confidence intervals across features—are inherited from the original paper. Our concerns are therefore limited to the new sign correction and to unavoidable changes in the reproduction.

Our main concern is that the proposed “Fixed ICA” rule is one reasonable way to make the evaluation less sign-dependent, but it is not yet established as the uniquely correct correction. The rule uses the 20 most extreme token activations from the evaluation corpus to orient each component toward its stronger tail. This makes orientation evaluation-dependent, and a stronger statistical tail need not be the more semantically coherent tail.

We suggest a few focused robustness checks:

1. Choose component signs using only the ICA fitting data, then keep those signs fixed during evaluation.
> We agree that ICA signs should be chosen using fitting data and then held fixed during evaluation. We reconstructed the exact fitting samples and applied the same stronger-tail orientation rule there. For the full-budget ICA models, fitting-data and evaluation-data orientation agreed for 1,747 of 1,800 components (97.1%), with 96.0%–98.0% agreement at every layer. Thus, the evaluation-dependent choice affects only a small minority of components, although we agree that fitting-data orientation is the cleaner protocol.

2. Report both tails of each ICA component separately. This would show whether the stronger tail is reliably the more interpretable one and whether both tails sometimes encode distinct concepts.
> We agree that interpreting both tails might be interesting. However, it is beyond the scope of our present claim. Our objective is not to establish that the stronger tail is uniquely correct or to optimize ICA’s score, but to show that the released one-sided evaluation depends on an arbitrary ICA sign and can therefore substantially understate ICA’s measured interpretability.

3. Test whether the result is robust to reasonable orientation rules, such as skewness, maximum magnitude, tail quantiles, or the strongest fragments rather than the strongest individual tokens.
> We agree that comparing alternative orientation rules would be useful for identifying a preferred general-purpose ICA orientation convention. Our narrower objective is not to optimize or uniquely justify an orientation rule, but to demonstrate that the released one-sided evaluation is sensitive to ICA’s arbitrary fitted sign and can substantially under-score ICA.

4. Repeat the comparison with another modern interpreter/simulator and, if feasible, multiple generations. The original GPT-4/GPT-3.5 pipeline is unavailable, so the replacement evaluator is necessarily a meaningful change rather than a purely mechanical compatibility update.
> We agree that replacing the retired GPT-4/GPT-3.5 pipeline with GPT-4.1-mini is a substantive evaluator change. To keep comparisons controlled, all methods in our reproduction use the same prompts, examples, scoring procedure, and interpreter model, and we do not compare absolute scores directly with the original figure. Our present claim is therefore conditional on this evaluator. Repeating the complete experiment with another interpreter and multiple generations would be valuable evidence of evaluator robustness, but it would require rerunning thousands of paid interpretation and simulation calls—approximately another $50 per complete evaluator run in our setup. We regard this as useful follow-up work rather than a prerequisite for demonstrating the sign-sensitivity of the released ICA evaluation.

5. Clarify two apparent paper/code discrepancies: Figure 2 describes ten SAE epochs, whereas the Pile10k reproduction uses 20,981,760 presentations (about 1.36 passes over its unique activations), and the paper describes approximately four million residual-stream activations for ICA, whereas the reproduction calls 2,098,176 activations the paper budget.
> Thank you for identifying this ambiguity. Our reproduction follows the released artifact path: ICA is fitted on the first 2 GiB float16 activation chunk, containing 2,098,176 512-dimensional residual-stream activations, while the SAE checkpoint used by the released interpretation code is saved after ten 2 GiB chunk-training stages. The paper describes the former as approximately four million activations and the latter as ten epochs, but these descriptions do not match the conventional counts implied by the released code. We will replace “paper budget” with “released-code budget” and report both activation presentations and unique-data passes explicitly.

We also recommend slightly narrowing the headline claim. The evidence clearly supports the conclusion that the original ICA measurement is not sign-invariant and can substantially under-score ICA. The current results further suggest that a stronger-tail ICA evaluation is competitive with SAE in later layers, while SAE remains clearly ahead in early layers. Describing the method as “a sign-invariant ICA evaluation” or “stronger-tail ICA,” rather than implying that it is the definitive correction, would accurately convey both the importance of the finding and the remaining uncertainty.

Overall, this is a valuable re-examination of an influential comparison. The proposed checks would strengthen the causal link between arbitrary ICA sign and the observed score increase, and would make the broader ICA-versus-SAE conclusion more robust.
